\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We introduce Challenge Run Value ($cRV$), a pitch-level metric for valuing
Automated Ball-Strike (ABS) challenges in Major League Baseball. Unlike
framing metrics, which measure receiving skill under human umpiring, $cRV$
treats the challenge as a scarce strategic resource: it combines
run-expectancy deltas from count- and plate-appearance-ending state changes
with a time-decaying, scarcity-weighted penalty for failed challenges. A
central contribution is the valuation of terminal outcomes, such as
challenges converting a called strike three into ball four (or the reverse),
which must be priced from post-event base/out states and runs scored rather
than zeroed. Applying the model to the $1{,}971$ plate-appearance-ending
challenges recoverable from the 2026 regular season through August 12, we
estimate a mean $cRV$ of $0.159$ runs per challenge (bootstrap $95\%$ CI
$[0.147,\,0.172]$); catchers generate roughly two-and-a-half times the
per-challenge value of batters ($0.231$ versus $0.088$). Zeroing terminal
states would instead drive the aggregate negative ($-0.016$), inverting the
sign of the result.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} run expectancy; opportunity cost; catcher framing;
sabermetrics; decision quality; resource allocation.

\section*{Data availability}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Data availability}
The pitch-level inputs are publicly available Statcast data, retrieved through
the \texttt{pybaseball} interface \citep{pybaseball}. The results reported in
this manuscript are computed from the 2026 regular-season challenge corpus
through August 12 ($n = 1{,}971$) extracted from those feeds. The deterministic synthetic
validation corpus described in Section~\ref{sec:validation} is retained as an
offline correctness harness and is regenerable from the accompanying code. All
analysis code, including the figure and table generation, will be made publicly
available on acceptance.

\section*{Figure legends}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Figure legends}

\noindent\textbf{Figure 1.} Five-module $cRV$ pipeline: ingestion, event
isolation, alternate-state generation, RE288 mapping, and scoring. Each stage
writes an inspectable intermediate file, so any challenge event can be traced
from its raw description text through to its final score.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Figure 2.} Player-level cumulative $cRV$ for the top
challengers of the 2026 regular season. Bars extending right of the zero rule
(blue) are positive and arise entirely from successful conversions; bars
extending left (red) are negative and arise entirely from opportunity-cost
penalties on failed challenges. Bar direction relative to the zero rule encodes
the sign independently of color, so the comparison survives grayscale
reproduction. The leaderboard is dominated by catchers, the primary users of
the challenge.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Figure 3.} Cumulative $cRV$ aggregated by challenger role
(batter, catcher, and a small number of events whose role could not be parsed).
Batters and catchers issue challenges in nearly equal numbers, but catchers
account for the large majority of aggregate value, converting borderline
two-strike calls into inning-ending outs.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Figure 4.} Total corpus $cRV$ as a function of the baseline
retained-challenge value $EV_c$, over the range $[0.034, 0.30]$ runs spanning
the empirical estimate and the fixed baseline. The relationship is exactly
linear because the opportunity-cost penalty applies only to failed challenges
and scales linearly in $EV_c$; successful challenges carry no penalty term.
Endpoint values are labeled.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Figure 5.} Total challenge win probability (cWPA) by challenger
role, the win-denominated counterpart of Figure~3. Successful overturns are
priced by their win-probability swing and failed challenges by a
win-denominated opportunity-cost penalty. As in runs, catchers accumulate the
large majority of win-probability value.
